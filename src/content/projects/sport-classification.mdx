## Overview

Sports are traditionally labeled "collision" or "contact." I led a team of three data
scientists testing that split against survey data on participants' behavior, cognition,
sleep, and concussion history.

## Approach

- Filtered the Sports Cog 1 survey (ages 18–25) from 360+ responses down to 332 valid
  participants, removing personal details and anyone who failed attention checks.
- Normalized free-text answers about sports, positions, sleep schedules, and concussions using
  regular expressions, plus the OpenAI API via LangChain for the hardest cases.
- Ran two-sample proportion tests (in Python and R) to find the sleep and attention responses
  that differ between people with and without a concussion history. This cut more than 150
  features down to 16: 9 sleep features and 7 attention features.
- Reduced dimensions with PCA, then compared k-means, hierarchical, and DBSCAN clustering using
  silhouette, Calinski-Harabasz, and Davies-Bouldin scores.

## Findings

- **Concussion history shows up in sleep and attention.** 45% of concussed athletes reported
  poor sleep, against 18% of other athletes. 21% said they "always" have trouble concentrating,
  against 2%.
- **Football carries an outsized burden.** It accounted for 21.4% of reported concussions while
  making up 9.6% of athletes.
- **Two clusters fit best.** All three scores agreed on k = 2 (silhouette 0.35). Clustering on
  the 16 significant features separated cleanly; random or full feature sets did not. That
  supports the traditional two-way split between collision and contact sports.
- **The labels don't map cleanly.** Sleep-based clusters matched convention best, especially
  for football. Attention-based clusters were the most cohesive (silhouette up to 0.81) but
  often put football and soccer players in the "contact" group. Changing the significance
  threshold could flip where football landed.

The traditional labels capture part of the picture, but not reliably. Future work should add
player positions (a kicker and a lineman face very different risks), EEG data, and better
coverage of collision sports beyond football.
