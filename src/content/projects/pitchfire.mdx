## Overview

Pitchfire is an AI-powered platform for both sides of a fundraise. Founders use it to
prepare and run their raise. Venture capital and private equity firms use it to source,
evaluate, and manage the deals that come in.

I'm a co-founder and the only engineer. I own the platform end to end: system
architecture, the full stack, AI systems, data infrastructure, the product roadmap,
releases, and production operations. When something ships, breaks, or needs to scale,
it's mine.

## Where it started

When I joined in December 2025, Pitchfire was an internal Python/AWS deal-flow management
and opportunity analysis tool built for venture capital firms. I rebuilt it as a multi-tenant, production TypeScript platform,
moved it to Replit, and widened it from a VC-only tool into a two-sided product: a
workspace for investment teams (now including private equity) and a workspace for the
founders pitching them. The AI layer evolved alongside it, from a local model to OpenAI
and then to Anthropic Claude.

## What I built

**For investment teams:**

- Automated deal intake from uploads, embeddable branded intake forms, DocSend links, and inbound email.
- AI analysis of every deck, plus teaser and CIM analysis for private equity, with deal type, EBITDA, revenue range, and sector filled in automatically.
- Thesis-match scoring from 0 to 10 against each firm's own criteria, with a breakdown of why a deal scored the way it did.
- Configurable pipelines with automatic advance or decline rules, team voting, NDA workflows, data rooms, and portfolio tracking.
- Co-investor intelligence: a catalog of thousands of external VC firms that powers deal sharing and syndication recommendations.

## Architecture

Pitchfire is one TypeScript codebase. The browser app is React 18 with Vite, TanStack
Query, Tailwind, and shadcn/Radix. The API is Node.js and Express. Both sides share the
same schema types and Zod validation contracts, so a field means the same thing from
the database to the form.

PostgreSQL, through Drizzle ORM, is the system of record for accounts, firms, deals,
analyses, pipelines, cap tables, and job state. I kept search inside it too: full-text
search runs on GIN indexes, and semantic matching runs on pgvector, so there's no
separate search or vector database to keep in sync. Files live in private object
storage and are served through short-lived signed URLs after a permission check.

I designed the data model around firm-level isolation. Every deal, document, message,
and pipeline record is scoped to its firm in the query layer. Access control runs on
four roles across founder, investor, and shared workflows, and the server checks them
on every request rather than trusting the UI. I've also spent real time on performance:
caching, redesigned joins and foreign keys, and removing N+1 queries to cut API, query,
and page-load latency.

On top of that pipeline I built:

- **Structured analysis.** Each deck gets a summary, traction highlights, key risks, a business-model breakdown, market research, a valuation range, and an investment recommendation.
- **An AI advisor panel.** Four distinct investor personas each evaluate a deal on their own, giving a conviction score, diligence questions, and a recommendation.
- **Embedding-based matching.** OpenAI embeddings in pgvector catch strong fits that keyword filters miss. Matching combines vector similarity with business filters and ranking logic, because a similarity score alone isn't a recommendation.
- **A permission-aware AI assistant.** It answers questions about the pipeline, deals, and the firm's criteria, and it can act: search deals, draft and send founder emails, move deals, and update criteria. Write actions appear as confirmation cards the user approves, are rate-limited and logged, and each firm can turn AI writes off with one switch.
- **Model routing and cost control.** Deeper analysis goes to a larger Claude model and lighter tasks to a smaller one, with prompt caching and usage tracking to keep quality up and cost down.

## Reliability

Most of the hard engineering is in what happens when things go wrong. Deck processing,
investor-catalog enrichment, news synthesis, and email delivery all run as asynchronous
pipelines, and I built each one to recover:

- Job state is persisted in PostgreSQL, so work interrupted by a restart is picked up by startup sweeps and stale-job detection instead of disappearing.
- Workers claim jobs through the database, which prevents two processes from doing the same work.
- Provider calls retry, duplicate articles and records are filtered out, and email delivery is reconciled against delivery events.
- A news pipeline polls RSS feeds and web search on a schedule, then deduplicates, embeds, clusters, quality-checks, and ranks articles into a feed personalized to each firm.

Around that sit database migrations, monitoring through logs, audit records and admin
diagnostics, and Vitest regression tests.

## Security

Investors trust Pitchfire with confidential deal flow, so security is part of the
design rather than a later pass. The platform has firm-scoped data access, role-based
permissions, two-factor authentication, bcrypt password hashing, Postgres-backed
sessions with hardened cookies, AES-256-GCM encryption for sensitive fields,
malware-scanned uploads, watermarked data-room downloads, rate limiting, schema
validation on every endpoint, and an audit trail for sign-ins, permission changes, and
admin actions.

## Connected workflows

The part I'm proudest of is how the pieces connect. A founder's deck analysis feeds
their investor matches. A match becomes outreach. Outreach lands in a firm's intake,
gets scored against its thesis, moves through its pipeline, and sends the founder a
status update automatically. Data rooms, NDAs, and cap-table data travel with the deal.
Making that work across different users and organizations, without leaking anything
between them, is what turns a set of features into a platform.
